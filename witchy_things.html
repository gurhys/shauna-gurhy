Wiccan Holidays

The Eight Sabbats

Seasonal notes for each point on the Wheel of the Year.

February 1-2

Imbolc

A threshold festival for the first signs of spring, fresh starts, hearth light, and quiet renewal.

  • Season: late winter
  • Themes: cleansing, inspiration, beginnings
  • Symbols: candles, snowdrops, milk, seeds

March 20

Ostara

The spring equinox, when day and night meet in balance and the growing season begins to gather strength.

  • Season: spring
  • Themes: balance, growth, fertility
  • Symbols: eggs, blossoms, hares, green shoots

May 1

Beltane

A bright fire festival for abundance, creativity, protection, pleasure, and the land coming fully alive.

  • Season: late spring
  • Themes: vitality, love, celebration
  • Symbols: bonfires, ribbons, hawthorn, flowers

June 21

Litha

The Summer Solstice Sabbat to celebrate and honour the sun. The longest day and the shortest night of the year.

Litha, also known as Midsummer, falls on the summer solstice around June 21 in the Northern Hemisphere — the longest day and shortest night of the year.

Across Celtic, Germanic, and Norse traditions, Midsummer was one of the great fire festivals. Communities lit bonfires on hilltops and gathered to dance, feast, and give thanks for the warmth and abundance of the season. Leaping through the flames was said to bring luck, courage, and protection, and the ashes were scattered on fields to bless the coming harvest. Sun wheels of straw were set alight and rolled downhill as a symbolic acknowledgement that the sun's journey had reached its height.

In Irish mythology, Litha is closely tied to the sun goddess Áine and the god Lugh, whose festival Lughnasadh follows six weeks later. It is also the time of the Oak King and the Holly King — twin figures representing the light and dark halves of the year, whose mythic battle at midsummer tips the balance toward the waning light. Litha is considered a liminal time when the boundary between the human world and the fairy realm grows thin.

Herbs gathered at midsummer — especially St. John's Wort, lavender, and vervain — were believed to hold especially potent magic. They were hung above doorways, dried for winter medicine, and woven into wreaths and posies as offerings. Sacred wells were visited, hawthorn trees decorated, and gratitude expressed for everything that had grown and flourished through the long bright days.

For modern pagans and witches, Litha is a time to harness the peak energy of summer — for spells of abundance, courage, and love. It is a moment of gratitude and joy, a celebration of light in full bloom, and a quiet acknowledgement that from here the days will shorten and the wheel will turn once more.

August 1

Lughnasadh / Lammas

The first harvest festival, marking ripening grain, practical gratitude, craft, skill, and shared bread.

  • Season: first harvest
  • Themes: harvest, work, generosity
  • Symbols: bread, grain, corn, sunflowers

September 22-23

Mabon

The autumn equinox, a second point of balance and a moment to gather, preserve, reflect, and give thanks.

  • Season: autumn
  • Themes: balance, gratitude, release
  • Symbols: apples, grapes, acorns, leaves

October 31

Samhain

A liminal festival for endings, remembrance, divination, ancestry, and crossing into the darker half of the year.

  • Season: final harvest
  • Themes: ancestors, endings, intuition
  • Symbols: pumpkins, apples, veils, lanterns

December 21

Yule

The winter solstice, the longest night of the year, celebrating rest, return of light, hope, and deep winter magic.

  • Season: midwinter
  • Themes: rest, rebirth, warmth
  • Symbols: evergreens, candles, holly, logs
